Common Trading Psychology Mistakes (And How a Written Plan Fixes Them)

Psychology · 7 min read

Trading is one of the few activities where the biggest obstacle usually isn't a lack of information — it's your own reaction to winning and losing. Here are the patterns that show up again and again, and why having rules decided in advance is the most reliable fix.

1. Revenge Trading

After a loss, there's a strong pull to immediately re-enter the market to "win it back." This isn't a rational trade decision — it's an emotional one dressed up as a rational one. Revenge trades are frequently larger and less carefully considered than a trader's normal position, which is exactly why they tend to compound the original loss rather than recover it.

The fix: A hard daily stop-loss that ends trading for the day removes the opportunity to revenge trade entirely — there's no terminal open to act on the impulse.

2. Overconfidence After a Winning Streak

A string of good days can feel like proof that "you've figured it out," which quietly encourages larger position sizes or abandoning risk rules "just this once." Ironically, this is often when the biggest single-day losses happen, because normal caution has relaxed.

The fix: Keep the same position sizing and stop-loss rules regardless of a hot streak. If anything, a fixed weekly withdrawal schedule helps here — profits get banked automatically instead of sitting in the account, inflating confidence and available risk.

3. "Just One More Trade"

Whether after a win or a loss, this is the thought that turns a planned trading session into an open-ended one. Sessions that run long tend to be lower quality — fatigue and emotional depletion set in, and decision quality drops even if the trader doesn't notice it happening.

The fix: Define your stopping conditions in advance — a target hit, a stop-loss hit, or a fixed number of trades — and treat all three as equally valid reasons to be done for the day.

4. Moving the Goalposts Mid-Session

A trader who set a 20% daily target might, after hitting it, decide "I'm feeling good, let's push for 30%." This quietly erodes the entire purpose of having a target in the first place — it turns a fixed rule into a suggestion.

The fix: Treat hitting your target the same way you'd treat hitting your stop-loss: as a signal to stop for the day, not a springboard for more.

Why Writing It Down Works

None of these fixes rely on having more willpower in the moment — they rely on having already made the decision earlier, when you were calmer. A written (or tracked) plan turns "should I keep going?" from an emotional question into a simple lookup: what does the plan say? That shift is small on paper but significant in practice.

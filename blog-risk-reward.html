Risk-Reward Ratio: Why It Matters More Than Win Rate

Risk Management · 6 min read

New traders often obsess over win rate — the percentage of trades that turn out profitable. It feels like the most natural measure of skill. But win rate alone says almost nothing about whether a trading approach is actually sustainable. Risk-reward ratio is the piece most beginners overlook.

What Risk-Reward Ratio Actually Means

Risk-reward ratio compares how much you stand to lose on a trade against how much you stand to gain if it works out. A 1:2 risk-reward ratio means you're risking $1 to potentially make $2. A 1:1 ratio means the potential gain and loss are roughly equal.

This single number changes what win rate you actually need to be profitable. At a 1:1 ratio, you need to win more than half your trades just to break even, before costs. At a 1:3 ratio, a trader can be wrong nearly two-thirds of the time and still come out ahead.

A Simple Example

Imagine two traders, each making 10 trades of equal size:

  • Trader A wins 7 out of 10 trades, but risks $20 to make $10 each time (a 2:1 risk-to-reward against them). Even with a 70% win rate, the losses from the 3 bad trades can outweigh the gains from the 7 good ones.
  • Trader B wins only 4 out of 10 trades, but risks $10 to make $25 each time. Despite losing more often than winning, the size of the wins relative to the losses can still leave Trader B net profitable.

This is why "I win most of my trades" isn't, by itself, evidence of a good approach — and why "I lose more than I win" isn't automatically a red flag either.

Why This Matters for Setting Targets and Stop-Losses

A daily target and a daily stop-loss are really just a risk-reward ratio applied at the account level rather than the single-trade level. If your stop-loss is set larger than your target is realistically achievable, you've effectively built a poor risk-reward relationship into your entire routine — a handful of stop-loss days can outweigh many target-hit days, even if you hit target more often than you hit stop-loss.

Finding a Ratio That Fits Your Style

There's no universally "correct" risk-reward ratio — it depends on your strategy's realistic win rate. A high-frequency approach with a high win rate can work with a smaller reward relative to risk. A more selective approach that wins less often generally needs a larger reward relative to risk to stay sustainable. The mistake is not knowing which one you're running, or setting targets and stop-losses without ever doing this math.

The Takeaway

Before asking "how often do I win?", it's worth asking "how much do I make when I win, versus how much do I lose when I don't?" A modest win rate with a strong risk-reward ratio can outperform an impressive win rate with a poor one — and understanding this relationship is what makes daily targets and stop-losses meaningful numbers rather than arbitrary ones.

Why You Should Withdraw Profits on a Schedule, Not "When You Feel Like It"

Money Management · 5 min read

It's easy to think of a trading account balance as "your money" the moment it goes up. But until profit is actually withdrawn, it's still exposed to every trade that comes after it — which means a good week can quietly be given back over the following one.

Paper Gains Aren't Real Gains

An account that grows from $1,000 to $1,500 hasn't "made" $500 in any permanent sense — that $500 is still sitting in an account subject to the same risk as the rest of the capital. A single bad session can erase it as quickly as it appeared. Regular withdrawals convert a percentage of that unrealized growth into something that no future trade can touch.

Why a Fixed Schedule Beats "Whenever It Feels Right"

Leaving withdrawal timing to feeling introduces the same emotional bias that affects trade-sized decisions. After a big win, it's tempting to leave everything in "to keep the momentum going." After a loss, it's tempting to leave everything in "to make it back first." Both instincts push in the same direction: don't withdraw. A fixed schedule — for example, a set percentage withdrawn every Friday regardless of how the week felt — removes that decision from an emotional moment.

A Simple Weekly Approach

One straightforward method: at the end of each trading week, withdraw a fixed percentage of that week's closing balance — commonly somewhere in the 20–30% range, though the right number depends on your goals — and let the rest continue compounding into the following week. This keeps growth going while steadily banking real, spendable profit outside the account.

What This Protects Against

  • A single catastrophic session. If most of your historical gains have already been withdrawn, one bad day can only threaten the smaller, more recent portion still in the account.
  • Lifestyle creep in the account itself. Without withdrawals, a growing balance can quietly encourage larger position sizes "because the account can handle it," which increases risk without a deliberate decision to do so.
  • Losing sight of actual progress. A number on a screen is abstract. Money that's been withdrawn and used, saved, or reinvested elsewhere is a concrete measure of results.

The Takeaway

Compounding is what makes a trading account grow. Withdrawing on a fixed schedule is what makes that growth mean something outside the account. The two work together, not against each other — treat regular withdrawals as part of the plan, not a distraction from it.
